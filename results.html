What these numbers rest on

Time, token and cost cells are computed from the per-trial usage records and are checked by the build against the paper at full precision. The Success column is different. The resource rows can be recomputed from data files; the denominators cannot all be. All five GPT-5.6 Luna sessions and all five Claude Fable 5.1 sessions are now on record, but three of the Luna sessions ended without a final report, through a timeout or an abort, and Fable trial 5 has no report either. Those outcomes are recorded by the authors rather than derived from a usage file, and they are listed, trial by trial, in the denominators file published with the data below. The build checks the Success column against that file, so an unreviewed change to a denominator still fails the build.

Reusing experience

Every trial so far started from nothing. These three studies let the agent keep what it learned. Experience is ordinary files: measurements, procedures, corrections and working scripts, written at the end of one execution and read at the start of the next. Each execution still begins with a fresh agent context, so the only thing that carries over is what was written down.

Two comparisons on this page are two separate clips, not one composed video: ring cycle 1 beside ring cycle 4, and the same model with and without the transferred store. Each pair starts at the same task second and runs at the same speed, so the shorter run simply finishes first. Play them together; each still states its own trial and real elapsed time.

One arm takes a ridged ring off its base and puts it back, five times over, in a single continuous session with GPT-6 Astra at high thinking effort. Each cycle starts a new agent process that reads the previous checkpoint and updates the object poses from what it can see now. The first cycle has nothing to read: its first insertion stalls, the agent lifts the part safely, measures an offset of about 9 mm between the centre of the grasped ring and the tool, and corrects for it. Later cycles reuse that corrected alignment procedure and check it against current observations.

What each column counts

  • Visual loop: acquiring images, geometric queries, visual tool runs, and the model response that comes straight after an image. That response often carries perception, planning and code generation together, so the split between this column and the next depends on where the images fall.
  • Remaining response: the same agent's model and client time outside the other categories, including reasoning, generation, queueing and transport.
  • Local tools: shell commands, reading the interface documentation, listing files, local Python, and process polling.
  • Action service: controller planning, movement, settling, and requests that were refused. It includes stretches when the robot is standing still.
  • Check: from the last action to the reporting boundary. Report: writing the final result.
  • Overlapping intervals are counted once. For towel folding the two arms' request logs are merged and concurrent intervals counted once.

Image-conditioned decisions dominate. Four pair assembly spends 30.6 of its 62.0 minutes in the visual loop; the six block tower has the longest visual loop of the block configurations at 18.4 minutes. Throwing is the exception, with only 1.9 minutes of action service and 12.7 minutes of remaining response, because most of that session is analysis after the object has landed.

Four pair assembly, from a human video

The agent watches one human demonstration video and then assembles eight 3D-printed parts into four pairs in the demonstrated order: a ridged sleeve over a broad round base, a hexagonal nut over a short post, a smooth sleeve over a stepped cylinder, and a long pin into a hollow tube. Nothing tells the agent which part mates with which; it has to read the relationships and the insertion motions out of the video and out of the scene in front of it. The parts are printed from AutoMate meshes, and the tightest pair leaves a nominal radial clearance of 0.74 mm.

Success criterionSuccess requires all four pairs to be assembled at the end, with pairs that were finished earlier still intact, and everything stable after the gripper opens and the arm withdraws.

4 of 5 trials succeeded. Over the successful trials the mean completion time was 54.3 minutes, ranging from 38.6 to 78.2, at an average of 22,255 thousand tokens and USD 27.41.

Six dice lie on the table with different faces upward. The agent receives a language instruction to turn them so that the upward face matches the goal, together with a goal photograph that fixes which face that is. The initial and target faces are balanced across predefined conditions. Nothing restricts where the dice end up or how they are rotated in the table plane, so the agent has to choose a grasp for each die that makes the required rotation reachable, and then release it so that it settles on the intended face.

Success criterionSuccess requires all six dice to show the requested number after placement. Positions and in-plane rotations are free.

5 of 5 trials succeeded, at a mean 42.9 minutes, ranging from 22.6 to 56.0, at an average of 18,460 thousand tokens and USD 22.52.

Targeted throwing

  • Targeted throwing2/2Targeted throwing (potato), trial 1 of 2: successTargeted throwing (potato), trial 2 of 2: success

A language instruction asks the agent to pick up a potato-shaped object and throw it into a stationary bowl. The required motion is a circular swing driven by one joint, with the other five joint targets held fixed and the gripper opening during the swing, so the outcome depends on the relationship between arm motion and release timing. This configuration uses a separate runtime with timed motion programs: the agent specifies joint offsets at time knots and a schedule for the gripper, and the executor interpolates the commands on a 50 Hz grid.

Success criterionSuccess requires the object to separate from the moving hand, travel through the air, and come to rest inside the target region, which here is the bowl.

Two recorded sessions, both successful, at a mean 22.8 minutes, from 22.2 to 23.3, at an average of 6,256 thousand tokens and USD 8.94. The throw itself completes an average of 13.9 minutes after the task is delivered, so about 40% of that time is the analysis and verification that follow it.

This is the smallest sample on the page. The paper prints this row as 2/2 in the same column as every other row, but its two sessions were selected rather than drawn from a planned batch, so this page treats the row as a recorded case and not as a success rate. Occlusion leaves the rising part of the flight and the apex unresolved, so the page says the object was released during the swing and came to rest in the bowl, and does not claim a measured trajectory.

How a trial was run and judged

How a trial was run and judged

One agent, one session

Every trial starts a fresh agent session with the interface documentation, the task instructions, the reference media and an empty workspace. Model weights stay fixed. Solutions and logs from earlier trials are not supplied.

Default configuration

GPT-6 Astra at high thinking effort, inside Codex CLI, for every Table 1 row.

Fixed start

Each trial begins from a documented physical reset and an initial scene drawn from a predefined set of placements. Model and experience comparisons use matched scenes with counterbalanced order.

Budgets

Each task has a fixed wall-clock budget and a fixed budget for observation and action requests, shared across configurations. Recovery inside those budgets is allowed.

What ends a trial

The agent reporting completion, the budget running out, or a reset or human intervention. A reset or intervention ends the autonomous trial. Physical failure, budget exhaustion, intervention and infrastructure interruption are recorded separately.

What counts as success

The physical outcome and the execution video, judged against criteria fixed before evaluation. The required assembly, support, orientation and fold relationships have to survive gripper release and arm withdrawal.

Completion time

Measured from the delivery of the task and its references to the final physical verification. It includes reference processing, model calls, local computation, sensing, motion and recovery. Time is reported over successful trials only, so a configuration with no successful trial has no time.

Tokens and cost

Tokens are the sum of input and output usage across every model request in the trial, including reasoning and retries, reported in thousands. Repeatedly submitted context is counted on each request, and cached input counts once inside that request's input total. Cost uses standard API rates with cached input priced as cached.

What is not reported

The paper describes 95% Wilson confidence intervals and partial-completion diagnostics but does not report them, and this page does not invent them.

† Throwing time includes the trajectory analysis and verification that follow the throw. The throw itself completes an average of 13.9 minutes after the task is delivered, so roughly 40% of the reported 22.8 minutes comes after the object has landed.

How AGP works

The loop

  1. Observe

    The agent asks the robot for an overhead image, a wrist image with aligned depth, or the current joint and end effector state. Captures arrive with calibration and timestamps and are saved into the agent's own workspace.

  2. Write a program

    The agent writes Python during the run to read those files: measure a part from depth, fit a surface, intersect a camera ray with a plane, triangulate a point seen from two views, or compose several robot calls into one step.

  3. Command motion

    The agent submits a target: either joint angles or an end effector position and orientation, plus a separate gripper opening. The robot runtime turns the target into joint trajectories under workspace and motion limits, using Mink for inverse kinematics.

  4. Check

    Each motion returns the measured state and the arrival error. The agent takes a new image when it wants one, and decides for itself whether the physical result matches its estimate.

  5. Revise

    A failed insertion can prompt a closer look, a new alignment estimate, or a different approach direction. The agent keeps going until it reports completion or ends the attempt inside the trial budget.

What the agent gets

  • A task specification: a human demonstration video, a goal photograph, a language instruction, or a combination, plus reference materials and completion criteria.
  • A documented robot interface with seven commands: state, status, help, frames, move_ee, move_joints and gripper.
  • A writable workspace where it can save images, scripts and notes for the duration of the trial.
  • Fixed budgets for elapsed time and for observation and action requests.

What the agent does not get

  • No task-specific or environment-specific training, and no simulation rehearsal.
  • No learned motion policy or skill library underneath it. The agent computes its own targets.
  • No solutions or logs from earlier trials, except in the experience studies, which are recorded separately.
  • No teleoperation. A reset or a human intervention ends the autonomous trial.

The platform

The robot is an I2RT YAM arm with six revolute joints and a parallel gripper over a tabletop workspace. A wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D405 supplies aligned colour and depth at 640 by 360 pixels, and a fixed overhead Logitech BRIO supplies rectified colour at 1920 by 1080. Bimanual towel folding adds a second arm expressed in the first arm's coordinate frame. Targeted throwing uses a separate runtime with timed motion programs, so that the swing and the gripper release can be scheduled together.

Agent self-report and human review

Most trials end with a report written by the agent, and every trial has a separate review of the final state and the execution video by a person. Four of the 87 counted runs ended with no report at all, so on those there is nothing on the agent's side to compare. Where both exist they can differ. On this page a disagreement means the agent stated an outcome that the review contradicted: the agent called a trial successful and the review called it a failure, or the other way round. Trials whose report could not be classified automatically are counted apart, under a label of their own, because that is a limit of the parser and not a judgement by the agent. The per-trial file published below carries both fields for every trial, plus a label saying which of the four cases applies, so the counts on this page can be re-derived exactly.

The pin that was never found

Four pair assembly, trial 3 of 5. GPT-6 Astra at high thinking effort, Codex CLI, right arm. 89.2 minutes, ended at the timeout.

Three pairs went together. The hollow tube was knocked over and stood back up twice. On the final insertion the gripper was already partly open when the arm moved, the long pin left the jaws, and the agent could not locate it again. It stopped and reported the failure rather than claiming the task was done.

Agent report: failure. Human review: failure. They agree.

Six cubes standing, then not

Six block tower, trial 2 of 5. GPT-6 Astra high, Codex CLI, left arm. 35.6 minutes.

The fourth cube went down out of alignment, and the agent regrasped and corrected it while the stack was still intact. The sixth level was beyond a straight-down reach, so it built the top two cubes on the table and carried them up as a pair. All six stood after release. The tower came down during the withdrawal that followed. The agent then measured where the cubes had landed, found one grey cube at about 0.70 m in the robot base frame against a 0.65 m radial grasp limit, and ended the attempt as unsuccessful with time and command budget still left.

Agent report: failure. Human review: failure. They agree.

This is why withdrawal counts as part of the task, and it is also where recovery stops: reobservation and regrasping fixed the earlier alignment error, but they could not reach a cube that had left the workspace.

One corner that stayed unfolded

Simultaneous towel folding, trial 3 of 5. GPT-6 Astra high, Codex CLI, both arms. 27.6 minutes.

The agent carried both short ends inward at the same time and staged the placements, which is what the demonstration asks for. Repeated corner slips earlier in the run, and a curled long-edge fold, left a loose triangular corner that is plain in the final overhead image.

Agent report: an approximate or partial match. Human review: failure. This is a difference of degree rather than a contradiction, and the trial is counted as a failure.

Programs the agent wrote while the task was running

The paper's appendix counts, across 65 recorded executions, 37 Python working files the sessions kept from a run, across 18 sessions: 29 for perception and geometry, 7 for composing robot calls, and 1 for drawing reference points into an image. The logs hold a further 135 shell events with inline Python across 48 sessions. One case can be followed all the way from a camera image to a physical motion.

From a wrist image to a commanded pose, and back again

  1. The input

    A wrist depth image of a pin held in the gripper.

  2. The program

    The agent wrote a script that samples triples of points to find a planar surface, then takes a singular value decomposition to get its normal.

  3. The extension

    It extended the same script to intersect an overhead pixel ray with that plane, estimate the tip of the pin from an assumed length, and express the tip offset in the gripper frame. A further extension computed a corrected target orientation and position.

  4. The command

    The program produced a horizontal target of roughly 0.51325 by -0.15820 metres. The next motion request used 0.5133 by -0.1582 metres with the computed quaternion.

  5. The answer

    That request came back with an arrival error. The agent adjusted the requested position and carried on with the insertion.

In another assembly run the agent wrote a triangulation script that turns a wrist pixel and an overhead pixel into calibrated rays in the robot base frame, returns the midpoint of their closest approach as the position estimate, and returns their separation as a consistency check. The accuracy of that estimate still depends on the pixel correspondences the agent chose and on the calibration.

This case comes from a four pair assembly run under a reduced interface that is not one of the five counted trials. It is shown because it is the one place where a program's output, the request it produced, the error that came back and the correction can all be followed in the record.

What the programs contribute to performance is not established. Settling that would need comparisons that hold the interface capabilities and the inherited experience fixed.

A different arm and a five-finger hand

One recorded demonstration on other hardware: a seven-joint P7 arm with a RealHand L6 dexterous hand in place of the six-joint arm and parallel gripper. The hand has five fingers and six control channels, covering thumb flexion, thumb abduction and one channel for each remaining finger. Observations come from a fixed overhead RealSense D435i and a wrist-mounted RealSense D435, both colour streams at 640 by 480 pixels and 15 Hz. The task is to pick a bottle off the table. The same observe, program, command, check loop drives it; what changes is the arm kinematics and the number of things the end effector can be told to do.

This is qualitative evidence of one grasp and lift, and nothing more. The paper reports no token or cost figures for it and does not name the model. The bottle slips during the holding that follows the lift, and this paragraph is the only place the page says so.

How this differs from earlier work

Two families of systems already put a language model next to a robot. In the first, the agent writes a program in advance, as code or as a computation graph, and the robot runs that program during execution; every runtime decision has to be expressed as a rule written before the uncertain observation arrives. In the second, the agent orchestrates learned skills or vision language action policies, choosing what runs next from the goal and from execution feedback, while motion generation stays inside whatever policy was selected. AGP keeps the agent itself in the decision loop for the whole task: it chooses what to look at, writes and rewrites programs while the task runs, computes its own motion targets, and changes approach after a physical result, with no separately trained action policy underneath. The paper positions this against classical geometric modelling with task and motion planning, against learned visuomotor and vision language action policies, and against the wider agent literature on tool use, memory and reflection, and treats those agent mechanisms as things that move the policy boundary rather than as separate machinery.

Limitations

What the paper says

  • Time and money. Mean completion time per successful trial runs from 21.4 to 54.3 minutes and mean inference cost from USD 7.46 to USD 27.41. Both are real barriers to deployment.
  • Coordinated deformable manipulation is the weakest case. Simultaneous towel folding is the lowest configuration at 3 of 5, and sequential folding costs 50.8 minutes and USD 24.14 on average.
  • Cost and time grow with task complexity, as the six block tower shows against the pyramid and the two towers, and a large share of the overhead is reviewing and verifying outcomes. About 40% of the throwing time comes after the throw.
  • Image-conditioned decision cycles take a large share of elapsed time, with perception, planning and code generation coupled inside single model responses. There is substantial room to cut observation and decision overhead and the cost of writing and transferring experience.
  • Model choice matters. On the same two pair assembly task, GPT-5.6 Terra reaches 1 of 5, GPT-5.6 Luna 0 of 5 and Claude Fable 5.1 3 of 5.
  • Experience reuse helps some tasks more than others. Two pair assembly shows limited gains in task time, and the ring study is one dependent sequence with no matched empty-experience control, so it cannot isolate a causal benefit.
  • Experience transfer rests on very few successful trials, which limits how much weight the conclusion can carry.
  • Recovery has limits. Withdrawal is part of the task: a tower that collapsed while the arm moved clear pushed one cube past the 0.65 m reach limit, and it could not be rebuilt.
  • What the agent-written programs contribute is not established, and would need comparisons controlling interface capabilities and inherited experience.
  • The throwing evidence is two sessions, and occlusion leaves the rising part of the flight and the apex unresolved.
  • The cross-embodiment evidence is qualitative: a grasp and a lift, followed by a slip during holding.
  • Safety. Inaccurate perception, faulty programs and inappropriate motion requests can damage equipment or injure people. This implementation separates the agent's reasoning from motion execution and enforces workspace and motion limits in the robot runtime. Working near people or in unfamiliar environments would need further safety evaluation, supervision and independent protective mechanisms.
  • Samples are small: five trials per configuration, two for potato throwing. The paper describes 95% Wilson confidence intervals but does not report them.

What this page does not show

  • Almost every clip is sped up, because the arm is stationary for most of a trial. Each clip carries its speed-up and the real elapsed time, and one whole run is published uncut at 1x on the watch page.
  • Clips are cut. Where a clip skips a stretch of the run, the caption gives the amount, or the clip carries an on-screen card that gives it.
  • Video is cropped for privacy and for legibility. Cropping removes parts of the lab and, in several runs, a second arm running an unrelated trial at the same time.
  • Showcase trials were chosen for what they illustrate, not sampled at random. Every caption states which trial of how many, the failures have their own section, and the trial browser lists all 87 counted slots. Several showcase clips deliberately use a trial near the middle of its row rather than the best one.
  • The supplementary comparison of two coding agents on pyramid construction is reported in the paper but not shown here.
  • Times, tokens and costs are the paper's own accounting. They are not a measurement of what the same task would cost on other hardware or at other prices.
